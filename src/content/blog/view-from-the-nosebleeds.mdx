Around 6am, somewhere above 19,000 feet, with a frozen water pack and legs that were done hours ago, I completely lost it in front of a sign.

That was the top of Kilimanjaro, and it was only day seven of a thirteen day trip that ended up being a lot dirtier, funnier, and weirder than one sunrise. It was me and two of my oldest friends, Kavi and Aaron. Seven days on the Machame Route, one day in Nairobi that turned into a laundry fiasco, and three days in the Masai Mara chasing the Big 5.

Somewhere along the way the altitude also gave me nosebleeds that followed me all the way into Kenya.

## Getting to Arusha

Thirty hours after leaving DFW, Kavi and I landed in Nairobi around 7am local time. Customs took over an hour, and then our driver was waiting to take us to Arusha, another five hours away. At that point I hadn't showered in about two days and really needed to brush my teeth. We met up with Aaron, the third musketeer, loaded up the car, and spent the drive fighting to stay awake so jet lag wouldn't wreck the next few days.

A couple of card games and a border check later, we were checking into our hotel for the night, 3Degrees in Arusha. Awesome stay with an amazing staff. After 27 hours of air travel, all three of us took very long showers.

For dinner we found George's Tavern. It wasn't as quick as we'd hoped, and the food took about half an hour, but I got the Kuku Paka. It's a Tanzanian dish that's basically a chicken coconut curry, and it came with chapati, which is a lot like Indian roti. It was amazing, and it reminded me of Indian food, which was exactly what I needed after a day of shitty airplane food.

Something I haven't mentioned yet: why land in Nairobi and immediately drive to some random city in Tanzania?

Because we were about to climb Mount Kilimanjaro. The very next day. Starting one of the Seven Summits the day after a travel day that insane is absolutely bonkers, and we were hyped anyway.

We picked the Machame Route, a six or seven day trek up to Uhuru Peak at 5,895m, a little over 19,000 feet. People call it the Whiskey Route because it's tougher than the easier options, and the payoff is some of the best views on the mountain. That was kind of the point.

The other route we seriously looked at was Lemosho. It's around 70km, takes seven or eight days, and a lot of people say it's the most beautiful way up. I'd recommend it to most hikers. We just didn't have the patience for a possible eight day trek.

Picking a route is basically the entire planning decision, so here's the rundown we put together when we were choosing.

**Machame.** About 62km over six or seven days, up the south side. It's the most popular route on the mountain, so you will have company at every camp. Steep in spots, a lot of climbing up and dropping back down, and the best scenery you can get without adding days. This is the one we did.

**Lemosho.** About 70km over seven or eight days, starting on the west side. Quiet for the first couple of days, beautiful the whole way, and the extra day on the mountain means more people make the summit. If you have the time, take this one.

**Shira.** Nearly the same path as Lemosho, except you drive to the trailhead at about 3,500m and start there. Skipping the walk up to that altitude is exactly the shortcut your body doesn't want, and altitude sickness shows up early on this one.

**Marangu.** The Coca Cola Route. Five or six days, the only route where you sleep in huts instead of tents, and the only one where you walk down the same way you walked up. Cheapest option and it sounds like the easiest, but it's short, you barely acclimatize, and more people turn around on it than on any other route.

**Rongai.** The only route that starts on the north side, near the Kenyan border. Six or seven days, drier than everything else, which makes it the one to take in the rainy season. The climbing is gentler and the trail is empty, but the views are flatter and more of the same.

**Umbwe.** Short, punishing, and close to straight up the south face in six days. Almost nobody takes it, and the people who do already know what they're doing at altitude. Not a first mountain.

**Northern Circuit.** The long way, nine days and around 98km looping across the quiet north side. More people summit on this route than any other, purely because you spend so long getting used to the air. It also costs the most, for the same reason.

The pattern is hard to miss. The more days you give the mountain, the better your chances. Almost everyone who turns around does it because of altitude, not because their legs gave out.

As dinner wrapped up, our guides came by for a predeparture meeting to answer every question we had and give us some much needed reassurance that we would not die. Our main guides were Freddy and Dickson, with an amazing team behind them.

<Callout title="Who we climbed with" icon="🏔️">
Kili Trails. Faza and the team did so much to get us here, with great communication and support the whole way, and as you'll see, they kept being awesome for the rest of the trip.
</Callout>

After the meeting we stopped at a pharmacy and a convenience store for some medicine and local cigarettes (incredibly ironic, I know), had a couple of Kilimanjaro beers, and crashed.

## Kilimanjaro

Seven days, one route, and a crew that took better care of the three of us than we deserved.

### Day 2: Machame Gate to Machame Camp

Jet lag woke us up early, which for once was actually useful. Pickup was at 8:30am, and by 7:30 we were packed and fed.

The team showed up right on time and we were on the road by 8:40, watching the landscape change on the drive to the base of the mountain and introducing ourselves to the 14 person crew that would be taking care of us for the next week. A couple of hours later we pulled up to the Machame Gate.

### Day 3: Shira Cave Camp

Today's goal was Shira Cave Camp. About 5km and roughly 900m of straight climbing. Fun, right?

We set out around 8am after another amazing breakfast from the team, which became a theme for the rest of the trek. We passed through foggy forest and into a completely new vegetation zone that was just stunning.

We were about half a kilometer from camp when two of the team members came rushing down to carry our day packs. I felt terrible, but they wanted to help any way they could and wouldn't take no for an answer. It showed how much they value hospitality and taking care of people. After what felt like ages (six hours or so), we walked into a camp that was already set up, with hot tea waiting for us. Being treated that well kept surprising us.

We ate, rested, and then the team called us to a meeting, which was confusing, because meetings were usually at dinner and it was 4pm. We walked out of our tents and found all 16 of them, guides included, standing there staring at us. I was a little taken aback. They just wanted to properly introduce themselves, and then they broke into song and dance. It was awesome. They sang Tanzanian songs, we joined in without knowing a single word, and it made an already amazing experience even better.

Afterward we went on a short acclimatization walk around camp to get our bearings for the harder day ahead. We saw a couple of the caves and learned how hikers used to sleep in them before tents were required. We also learned how much the mountain has changed over the years, from caves collapsing to less and less snow every year. It's crazy seeing the effects of something most people shrug off, in person.

On a lighter note, we ate some bomb food and checked out the kitchen tent to see what the team does every night. They were playing a card game called Last Card, which is basically Uno, and they asked us to join. We spent hours laughing and playing with them before bed. Getting to see how they have fun in such a remote place with the bare minimum made for an amazing night.

### Day 4: Lava Tower to Barranco Camp

This was one of the harder days, about 10km, mostly because of the weather. It started off chilly, then it rained, and then it got cold as hell. The weather kept flipping, which made the first half a painful uphill trek.

We made it to Lava Tower (4,630m, and a pretty sick name) and had lunch and a cigarette with the team, strictly to warm up, of course. Then we headed back down to Barranco Camp at 3,960m. Going up and then back down is on purpose. It helps you acclimatize, and it gets you around the mountain to the easier climbing side.

### Day 5: Barranco Wall to Karanga Camp

By now we had a set routine: up around 6, hiking by 8. Today was no different.

The goal was to climb over the Barranco Wall and reach Karanga Camp, which sits at around the same altitude, in time for lunch. The wall was pretty sick. It's only about half a mile of distance, but it goes straight up this massive rock face and takes some real climbing, which was fun.

The top of the wall had breathtaking views of Kilimanjaro and the sea of clouds below, and we spent a stupid amount of time taking cool and dumb pictures until the guides told us we had to go.

Karanga is where you get a choice that can shorten the trip. You can eat lunch fast and push on to base camp, another four hours away, or stay the night. Honestly, we probably could have made it, but it would have made summit day miserable, and we didn't want to deal with the logistical nightmare of booking another hotel night for the extra day. So we stayed and chilled. That gave us a whole afternoon to relax, and luckily it was another clear day where we could just admire the views all around us.

Same wake up, same start time, heading up to base camp at Barafu Camp, 4,673m.

We tried to nap, but it was hot enough that the tent felt like a sauna, so we hung out outside where the wind made it cold. We were confused and tired, and my head was starting to hurt from the altitude, so we gave up and watched a movie in the dining tent.

Dinner was at 6:30. Bedtime was 7:30. The summit push started at midnight.

### Day 7: Summit night

A short nap later we were up at 11pm and getting ready to climb to the summit for sunrise. We were barely awake and knew we had to eat something, because this was about to be hard as hell. A piece of toast and some tea was all I could force down, and shortly after we were hiking up the mountain.

It's about 5km to the top, but roughly 1,200m of climbing. It was hell. We took so many breaks, and it kept getting colder the higher we went. Around halfway, we were truly questioning how we were going to make it all the way up. And it just kept getting worse. Our legs were shot, our water packs froze, and the little food we had was rock hard. But we kept going, and our guides kept encouraging us to keep going. It was so hard to stay positive, so we just stayed quiet and kept walking.

Six painful hours later we made it past Stella Point, and the sun was starting to creep up over the horizon.

<Figure src={summitSunrise} alt="The sun creeping over the horizon near the top." caption="The sun creeping over the horizon near the top." />

<PullQuote>
I was starting to get emotional as we got closer and closer to the summit. Maybe it was exhaustion from the lack of sleep, or the insane burn in my legs, or just everything it took to make this entire trip happen, but as we approached the summit sign I couldn't hold it in. I was overwhelmed. Being able to summit the tallest mountain in Africa with two of my lifelong friends, who came just because they wanted to support a goal I'd mentioned, meant the world to me. It's pretty awesome to have friends who are willing to do the stupid shit that I like to do.
</PullQuote>

The moment got cut short, because we had to head back down to avoid altitude sickness. Going down was somehow worse. The ground is loose, rocky sand, so every step slid a couple of feet. It absolutely killed my quads and shins, and a painful two hours later we made it back to base camp.

You'd think that would be it for the day, but no. We got about two hours to rest and eat a small snack before hiking down the mountain another four hours.

My nap was incredibly uncomfortable because the tent got hot again, and all that time at high altitude made my nose bleed a couple of times. Those nosebleeds kept coming for the next few days. On top of no sleep, none of us had any appetite. We couldn't even think about food. We just wanted to lie horizontal for a while. But, alas, the hike began.

Thank the lord the weather had cooled down, because it made the hike incredibly easy. Or so we thought. Halfway down, the trail turned questionable. It was incredibly rocky, we had to watch every step, and it was all downhill. Once again, the shins and quads were in shambles. Even with the weather and an easier trail on our side, we were dead. We took multiple breaks to stretch while our bodies fell apart.

By the time we were completely out of positivity and wanted to cut our legs off, we reached Mweka Camp, and it was so serene. It sits in the middle of the woods, with no wind and a mist that kept everything cool. An absolutely lovely camp. We ate dinner and were asleep before the sun even set.

### Day 8: Mweka Gate to Nairobi

We started at 5am on the last day to beat the park traffic. The hike out was fun and muddy, and we finished our insane seven day journey at the Mweka Gate around 9am. Honestly, the only thing any of us could think about was a shower. This was probably the longest I've ever gone without one.

We got our certificates of completion, which was really nice, and headed back to 3Degrees in Arusha, where we'd stayed before the climb. It was just a pit stop. Faza had arranged a room for a couple of hours so we could shower before heading to Nairobi that afternoon.

## Nairobi

### Day 9: Nairobi and the laundry fiasco

The day in Nairobi started a little more stressful than we'd have liked. I woke up at 6am to an email from SafariLink, the airline flying us to the Masai Mara the next day, saying our flight had been canceled because the airstrip was under maintenance. That's a reason I'd never heard before, but it made sense later in the trip. Their customer service team made it easy to find an alternative, and within the hour we had a different flight to the safari.

Next order of business was laundry, but cheap. The hotel wanted damn near $50 to $75 for a single load. Absolutely not. I looked around at local laundromats and found one down the road for about a quarter of the price. Still pretty bad, but I needed clothes, especially since I'd run out of underwear. After getting lost on the way and ending up on the wrong side of the building, I finally handed over my laundry, and the nice lady told me to come back around 5 or 6 to pick it up.

<Callout title="Laundry in Nairobi" icon="🧺">
Hotels charged us $50 to $75 a load. A local laundromat a few minutes away was about a quarter of that. Just write down their closing time, which is a lesson I learned the hard way.
</Callout>

At this point we were hungry, so we walked about five minutes from the laundromat to a place I'm pretty sure was just called Coffee and Bagels. While I was waiting for my food, I felt that little tickle in my nose, and before I knew it my entire hand was full of blood. One trip to the bathroom later, it was a great meal, to say the least.

The bagel was very fresh. I ordered a brisket, egg, and cheese bagel sandwich with a little omelet on the side, and it was one of the best bagels I've ever had. I've been to New York. I've been to Chicago. I've been to all the bagel famous places, and one of the best bagels of my life was in Nairobi, Kenya. Maybe that was pure bias after a week without a normal meal, but at the time, it was up there with the best.

Then we went to a mall in northern Nairobi that was set up in a really interesting way. It's partially outdoors until you reach a little patio area in the middle, and the whole thing feels like a maze, with every wing full of stores I didn't expect to find there. We hung out at small cafes and chocolate shops and checked out local brands, mostly just to kill some time. I also picked up a nasal spray for my nose. Life changing. A couple of uses and I was good to go.

After the mall we decided we finally deserved a massage. Our legs and backs were sore and we were incredibly tired. We stopped at Iconz Spa and got an unreal full body massage. We were treated like kings and even got to shower afterward. The whole experience was about 50 bucks, which I can't even complain about.

We were so relaxed and clueless that we just wandered for a while. We grabbed some alcohol for the next couple of days and stopped at an Indian restaurant for one last peaceful dinner in the city.

That's when the laundry fiasco began.

At 5:30pm I got a call from the laundromat lady saying she closes at 6 and I needed to come pick up my clothes. We were over 45 minutes away, so there was no way to get there in time. I tried to propose a fix. The laundromat was about two blocks from our hotel, and they offered delivery anyway, so could they just drop it off there? I'd pay extra if I needed to. She was super nice, but bless her heart, she could not understand me at all. It took 15 minutes of explaining before she finally got it and sent a driver to the hotel with my laundry.

The next step was getting the hotel to pay the driver, since I wasn't there. The hotel refused. Of course they did, otherwise it would have been too easy. So I spent the next 30 minutes figuring out how to pay a driver who had already left my clothes at the front desk and trusted me to pay him later. It didn't help that during this entire time I was in the bathroom, dealing with another damn nosebleed.

In the end it worked out. I paid him through some app that charged me an extra 10 bucks in transaction fees. Easily the most insane laundry experience of my life, but thank god I got my clothes back, or the next couple of days would have been rough.

We got back to the hotel, had a couple of beers, played some cards, and got in bed. We had an early morning and didn't want to be tired for our first day in the Masai Mara.

## The Masai Mara

Little did we know we'd get a 12 seat plane with only two other passengers, so we sat right up front next to the pilots. This was an awesome experience. It was about an hour to the airstrip, and we saw so many animals on the way: zebras, giraffes, and one rhino, which was insane, and I was pretty proud of myself for spotting it.

## Heading home

### Day 13: Back to Nairobi and back to Dallas

Well, I woke up feeling great, while the other two were throwing up and shitting themselves right up until we left for the airstrip.

It was a brutal flight back to Nairobi, with the plane landing at two other airstrips before we finally got to Wilson Airport. To make things worse, our bags took ages to come out, so we were stuck waiting about 30 minutes while both of them felt like absolute hell.

After an excruciatingly long time, we got a cab to Aaron's hotel for the night. We got there and both of them immediately crashed. I watched TV and ordered some food while they recovered a little. About six hours later they felt decent enough to get out of bed and eat something, so we did. But after a couple slices of toast, they went straight back to bed until it was time for Kavi and me to head to the airport.

A pretty uneventful last day, but I didn't mind, and I'm sure they didn't either. 26 hours later, Kavi and I made it home, and it took me about a week to fix my sleep schedule.

## What stuck with me

**Who you go with matters more than where you go.** The views were unreal, but the parts I keep replaying are the three of us: card games on the drive to Arusha, the dumb pictures on top of the Barranco Wall, and summit night, when nobody had the energy to talk and we just kept walking next to each other. A hard trip with the right people turns into the best stories. The same trip with the wrong people is just hard.

**The crew is the trip.** Fourteen people carried our gear, cooked every meal, had camp set up and tea ready before we even arrived, ran down to grab our day packs half a kilometer from camp, sang for us, and dealt us into their card games at night. I went to Tanzania to climb a mountain. What I actually think about is the people who got us up it.

**Take the rest day.** At Karanga we could have pushed straight to base camp and saved a day. We didn't, and halfway up the next morning with our legs on fire, I was glad about it with every step. The faster plan would have had us starting summit night already wrecked.

**Go in with no expectations.** We showed up to the Masai Mara expecting zebras and saw a black rhino before lunch on day one. It's the same thing I learned in Maine. The less you've decided a place is going to be, the more room it has to surprise you.

**The guide beats the organized stop.** The walking tour at the Mara Conservation Center was fine. Derrick reading tracks, birds, and wind to find a leopard after six hours was the best thing we did in the park. If you're picking where to spend money on a safari, the driver matters more than almost anything else.

**The cheap option still won, even when it fought back.** I refused to pay the hotel $50 to $75 to do my laundry. That turned into a missed closing time, a hotel that wouldn't pay my driver, and a $10 app fee, all handled from a bathroom in the middle of a nosebleed. It still came out way cheaper, and I got my clothes back. I'm counting it.

Aaron and Kavi, thank you. Neither of you ever planned on climbing Kilimanjaro. You came because it was something I wanted to do, and you put up with an itinerary that had us starting the climb the day after 30 hours of travel, seven days without a shower, and a last day in Nairobi spent sick in a hotel room. Somewhere in there we still found time for card games in the car, handstands above the clouds, and a sundowner in a car that barely ran. I couldn't have done this with anyone else, and I wouldn't have wanted to.

The three of us pulled off another trip of a lifetime, and the goal from here is to make each one a little crazier than the last. For some context, our first one was August 2024, a run through Vietnam, Singapore, and Indonesia. That blog is coming soon too.
