We did Japan in a little under three weeks, two of us, with one set of flights booked and almost nothing locked in between them. Tokyo, Hokkaido, Hiroshima, Onomichi, Kyoto, Osaka, Okinawa. We knew the order and we knew the dates we had to be on a plane. The rest we figured out as we went.

This isn't a highlight reel. There are enough of those, and most of them point you at the same fifteen places. What I actually want to write down is what the trip surfaced about how to travel well without spending like a tourist — because the longer we were there, the clearer it got that the best parts of the trip and the most expensive parts of the trip were almost never the same thing.

A few of the days were chaos. We missed trains, had an Uber cancel on us, sprinted through an airport, and stood in front of an unstaffed check-in desk reading a sign about a typhoon. I'll get to those, but only briefly, because they're not the point. The point is the inn outside Kyoto that cost seventy dollars and beat the reservation-only omakase in Tokyo. The walk-in tempura counter in Kushiro run by one guy. The cycling route nobody recommended except one friend who'd actually lived there.

Here's roughly where we went and in what order. The back-and-forth is real — we did not optimize this.

## Tokyo

We gave Tokyo about three and a half days. Getting from the airport to our Airbnb the first night was a mess — a late train, a bag stuck in customs, twenty hours of travel already behind us — and we didn't get in until well past when we'd planned. We dumped our stuff, walked Shinjuku and Shibuya for a couple of hours anyway, ate soba at a place called Ramen Torisoba, and forced ourselves to stay up until eleven to kill the jet lag before it killed us.

The next day was the tourist day, and that's fine — some of the famous stuff is famous for a reason. The Imperial Palace and its gardens in the morning, then shrines: Meiji Jingu, Senso-ji. By four we'd switched into shopping mode and didn't stop until the stores closed — Muji, Onitsuka Tiger, Uniqlo, a few Tokyo-only spots, a lot of time spent standing in genuinely absurd lines.

<Figure caption="Placeholder: Shibuya or Shinjuku at night — neon, crowds, the crossing" />

That night we had the reservation we'd been looking forward to: an omakase at Sushi Hajime in Shibuya. It was very good. I want to be clear about that. But it wasn't close to the best meal of the trip, and the fact that it was the meal we'd planned around is exactly the thing I kept thinking about for the next two weeks. The last day we kept loose on purpose — Ginza, the electronics district, camera bits, a pastry place called Age 3 — mostly just wandering with no plan, which in hindsight was the best Tokyo day of the three.

## Hokkaido

Hokkaido is where the trip got both harder and better. Day one was the most stressful stretch of the whole three weeks: a 7:30 flight, two missed trains, an Uber that cancelled, too many suitcases, and a security line we cleared right as boarding started. We made the flight on Japanese efficiency alone. I won't dwell on it — it worked out, and it became a good story, which is most of what those days are for.

Once we had the rental car, the island opened up. We sat by Lake Shikotsu for an hour, then drove three and a half hours to Furano. The next day was Daisetsuzan National Park and the top of Mt. Asahidake, walking around geothermal vents at the summit, then over to Sounkyo Gorge for the Ryusei and Ginga waterfalls — a completely different landscape a couple of hours apart.

<Figure caption="Placeholder: geothermal vents at the summit of Mt. Asahidake" />

Then there was Abashiri. We took our time getting there — a local fox farm, farmers' markets, and a cape called Notoro Misaki that I loved, almost empty, with trails out past a lighthouse where we ended up napping in a field. (My friend left an expensive drone somewhere in that field. We never found it. Build slack into the plan.) Dinner in town was at Yoshida Mihachi Shouten — it's on my Beli if you want specifics.

The Airbnb that night is one I want to come back to. It was a handmade home built by local people — earthen, full of natural shapes and outdoor-facing spaces, modern where it counted. The kind of place you remember the building itself, not just the view.

<Figure caption="Placeholder: the earthen, handmade Airbnb in Abashiri — exterior (this is the one called out in the notes)" />

The last full Hokkaido stretch was Shiretoko. We had to choose between a delayed forest walking tour and a nature cruise — bear sightings and weather had scrambled the schedule — so we did the forest walk first (five lakes, decent, a little underwhelming after everything else) and rescheduled the cruise for the next day. The cruise was worth the juggling: a full pod of orcas and whales in the distance, even in heavy rain. The Airbnb there had a private onsen in the backyard, which is where most of that evening went.

<Figure caption="Placeholder: orcas on the Shiretoko nature cruise" />

The night before we flew out, in Kushiro, we stumbled into what might be the best meal of the trip. A tempura omakase called Tempura Ryori Sakura — one guy, no reservation, who waved us in anyway, poured us a local sake, and ran us through ten to fifteen courses of locally sourced seafood and meat. We lost count. It cost a fraction of the Tokyo omakase and it wasn't close.

<Callout title="Best meal of the trip" icon="🍤">
Tempura Ryori Sakura, Kushiro. One chef, no reservation, ten-to-fifteen courses of local seafood. Walk-in. The most memorable meal we had, and far from the most expensive.
</Callout>

## Hiroshima

Mostly a travel day, and I'll treat it like one. Three or four hours in the car to New Chitose Airport, a flight south, a bus to the hotel, Japanese barbecue downtown, early night. Hiroshima was the hinge between the north and one of the best days of the whole trip, which was the next morning.

## Onomichi

Onomichi was the day I'd point a budget traveler to first, and it's the one almost nobody told us about. One friend — who'd actually lived in Japan for years — mentioned that this small town east of Hiroshima runs on a cycling economy, and that was the whole recommendation. No listicle, no guide. Just a person who knew.

We took an early Shinkansen, dropped bags at Hotel Cycle, and picked up a commuter bike I'd reserved a month or two ahead. The Shimanami Kaido is about 70 km across six islands; we did roughly half, three islands, over bridges, through forest and small villages, stopping for ice cream and fresh roadside lemonade. Mukai Shima first, mostly urban; then a climb to the Innoshima bridge and the best stretch — jungle, forest, tiny villages; then over to Ikuchijima, fast descents, ending at Setoda Port with its sunset beach and the Kosanji temple.

<Figure
	wide
	caption="Placeholder: bikers crossing one of the bridges on the Shimanami Kaido route"
/>

<Callout title="If you want to ride it" icon="🚲">
Reserve the bike a month or two ahead, especially in season — I booked from Onomichi and it was tight. We started at 10am and did half the route comfortably. To do the full 70 km to Imabari, start at 6am and bus back. That's the plan for next time.
</Callout>

The town itself is full of small shops, and you meet cyclists from all over who came for the same thing. We finished the night at Hotel Cycle over local lemon craft beer — the whole area is lemon-obsessed and it works — and a long conversation that ran a couple of hours. It's an easy day to recommend and a hard one to forget.

## Kyoto

More early starts — we left at 5:45 to catch the Shinkansen. We picked up a rental car in Kyoto and spent the day on the temples: Nanzen-ji, Toji, then out to Arashiyama. The bamboo grove is genuinely worth seeing and also the one place on the trip I'd warn you about — it was packed, and the crowds were the only real downside. Go early or go off-season. Same place, very different day, depending on the hour you pick.

<Figure caption="Placeholder: Arashiyama bamboo grove (go early — this is what crowds look like otherwise)" />

With the car, we kept going to Kayabuki no Sato, a small thatched-roof village out in the middle of nowhere, and that's the part I'd actually send you to. The drive there was beautiful; the drive back, through an emptied-out stretch of Kyoto — abandoned towns and temples, then a two-way "highway" barely wide enough for one car through the forest — was the scariest and most fun stretch of road on the trip.

We ended that night at Ryoso Chatani. On paper it's a two-star hotel that cost us about seventy dollars. In practice it was the best stay of the trip. An elderly couple and their son run it; they called us down at dinner and cooked us hot pot, we bathed in their own onsen, and we slept on tatami mats. They didn't speak much English and did everything they could anyway. This is the whole thesis of the trip in one booking — the cheapest room we paid for was the one I'd go back for.

<Figure caption="Placeholder: Ryoso Chatani — the family inn, hot pot dinner or the tatami room" />

<Callout title="Where we stayed" icon="🏠">
Ryoso Chatani — roughly $70/night, family-run, home-cooked hot pot, private onsen, tatami mats, outside central Kyoto. Books out fast; reserve months ahead.
</Callout>

After dinner we drove out to Fushimi Inari Taisha — the thousand torii gates everyone's seen on Instagram. At night it's a different place entirely: almost empty, quiet, the climb to the overlook of Kyoto lit up below. Same gates, same price (free), completely different experience because of the hour. If you only do one thing in Kyoto after dark, do this.

<Figure
	wide
	caption="Placeholder: Fushimi Inari at night — the torii gates, nearly empty"

If I did it again I'd give Kyoto two or three nights instead of squeezing it. We'll be back, probably off-season.

## Osaka

Osaka was the one fully planned splurge day, and I'm glad we did it. After dropping the rental car and sorting out a transit card that refused to work, we checked into the Candeo Tower Hotel and went straight to Universal Studios Japan. It was raining, which actually helped — we'd bought tickets with eight fast passes, and the combination of rain and skip-the-line meant most rides were ten to fifteen minutes, tops.

<Figure caption="Placeholder: USJ in the rain, or the rooftop infinity pool at the Candeo Tower" />

A few rides were closed for the weather and Nintendo World was fine but oversold; the real fun was Harry Potter world and the Hollywood section, pure childhood nostalgia. We ended the night on the hotel's rooftop — matcha milks that turned out to be alcoholic, then the highest infinity pool in the world, saunas, indoor and outdoor pools at different temperatures. After two weeks of go, go, go, it was the right way to stop moving for a night.

<Callout title="USJ, efficiently" icon="🎢">
Buy the multi-pass with fast passes — we had eight — and go on a low-attendance day if you can. A rainy day plus fast passes got us through nearly everything we wanted with almost no waiting.
</Callout>

## Okinawa

Getting to Okinawa started with a scare — we showed up to an unstaffed Jetstar check-in desk and a sign about a typhoon cancelling flights. Ours wasn't listed, so we waited it out with a few other confused passengers until staff finally arrived. Then a short flight, a rental car, and a hidden Airbnb in the forest near Nanjo on the eastern side of the island, with a host who showed us around himself. Takoyaki and udon for dinner, snacks for the morning, and an early night before the part of the trip I'd been most nervous about: our first real day of diving since getting certified.

<Figure caption="Placeholder: the forested Airbnb near Nanjo, Okinawa" />

The diving was the best thing we did. I was nervous — everyone else on the boat looked like they knew exactly what they were doing — but our guide, George, made it fun instead of frightening. We dove off the Kerama Islands: massive coral, swim-throughs, small caves, a whole day of getting comfortable with the gear and our buoyancy.

<Figure

/>

<Callout title="Who we dove with" icon="🤿">
Sunkissed Divers, Okinawa. Guide was George (with his assistant Taron on day two). Patient with first-timers, ran the Kerama Islands. If you just got certified, this is the kind of operator you want for your first open-water days.
</Callout>

The second dive day was even better. Cloudy, humid start that cleared the second we got on the boat, George and his twenty-year-old assistant Taron cracking jokes the whole way out. We saw turtles, sea snakes, slugs, anemones, more reef fish than I could name — and spent ten minutes and way too much air bouncing around a huge sand patch like astronauts on the moon.

<Figure caption="Placeholder: a turtle over the reef, Kerama Islands" />

That second day is also where the trip handed me the thing I keep coming back to.

## What stuck with me

A few lessons, said plainly, because leaving them implied would be a cop-out.

**The most expensive thing you book is rarely the best memory.** The Tokyo omakase we planned around was good and forgettable. The seventy-dollar family inn outside Kyoto and the one-guy tempura counter in Kushiro were the two things I'd fly back for. If you're traveling on a budget, that's not a consolation prize — it's the actual strategy. Spend your attention, not your money.

**Friction is part of it.** The Hokkaido morning and the Okinawa typhoon desk were stressful in the moment and became the stories I tell first. You can't optimize a trip into being memorable. Leave slack in the plan and let some of it go sideways.

**Locals beat listicles.** Onomichi came from one friend who'd lived in Japan, and it was the best day of the trip. Arashiyama came from everyone, and it was the most crowded. Same country, two completely different outcomes, depending on who you listen to.

**Timing is a free upgrade.** Fushimi Inari at night versus during the day is the same place at the same price and a totally different experience. Picking the hour is the cheapest improvement you can make to almost anything.

And then there's the one I didn't expect, which came up underwater on the last dive:

<PullQuote>
A lot of my activities over the last couple of years have been intense, or at least require some level of vigilance — a hard hike, climbing up a steep boulder. But this was pure meditation. Being down there watching these creatures glide through the water while you breeze past is so calming. A great reminder to look around and smell the roses every once in a while — something I constantly forget on trips like these, where I'm trying to absorb everything as fast as possible, afraid to miss something I might not get back to. But there's something beautiful in the patience, and I think it's underrated. Yeah, you might miss some of what a place has to offer, but the vastness of these places, and the fact that they're always changing, is exactly what makes them so exciting.
</PullQuote>

That's the whole trip, really. We moved fast, we spent little where it counted, and the best moments were the ones we almost rushed past. I'll be back — off-season, slower, with a little more Japanese — and I'll still be looking for the seventy-dollar room over the one with the reservation list.
